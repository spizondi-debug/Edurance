How to study for matric exams: a plan you can actually follow

You do not need a perfect timetable. You need a repeatable way to practise, check what went wrong and keep moving.

Published 27 September 2026 · EduRance team

Matric preparation can feel like several exams arriving at once. Start by writing down your subjects, the topics you find difficult and the papers you will write. Then check the official Department of Basic Education exam page for your timetable. Save a copy of the latest version; do not plan around a screenshot shared without a source.

1. Make a short, realistic weekly plan

Give each subject a place in the week, then put extra sessions where you need the most practice. A useful session has a clear task: “solve four trigonometry questions” works better than “study Maths.” Leave room for school, travel, meals, sleep and a catch-up session. If you miss a day, move one task; do not throw the plan away.

  • Start: name one topic you need to improve.
  • Practise: work on questions without reading the memo first.
  • Check: mark your answers and write down the mistake.
  • Return: retry the same type of question a few days later.

2. Use past papers as practice, not as reading material

Download the DBE past papers and memos for the subjects you take. Begin with one section at a time. When you are more confident, try a full paper under timed conditions. The memo tells you how marks were awarded, but the real learning happens when you correct your own answer.

Need help finding the right paper and using its memo? Follow our step-by-step matric past papers guide for the official archive and a practice method.

Keep an error list with three columns: question or topic, what went wrong and how I will solve it next time. If you repeatedly miss the same idea, ask a teacher or classmate for an explanation, then try another question on your own.

3. Test what you can remember

After reading a section, close the book and explain it aloud or write the main points from memory. Check what you left out. For calculations, work through a fresh problem. For essays, practise making a short plan and supporting each point with evidence. This shows you what you can actually use in an exam.

4. Give difficult subjects a smaller first step

If Maths or Physical Sciences feels overwhelming, start with one worked example, one similar question and one independent question. The DBE's 2025 diagnostic reports discuss common mistakes in past matric papers; use those mistakes to decide which topics deserve another practice session. Avoid spending the whole evening rewriting notes you already understand.

5. In the final days before a paper

Check the venue, start time, permitted materials and your transport plan with your school. Revise your error list and practise a few representative questions. Make space to sleep and eat. If exam pressure feels hard to manage, tell a trusted teacher, parent or caregiver; you do not have to handle it alone.
